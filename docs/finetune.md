title: Fine-Tuning Vision Models nav_order: 3<div class="bs-hero"><h1>Fine-Tuning Vision Models</h1><p>Making generic vision models "chess-aware" through specialized training strategies.</p></div>We transform general-purpose visual encoders into specialized chess tile classifiers. By fine-tuning on our custom dataset, we move beyond generic feature extraction to high-precision piece recognition.{: .repro }The training pipeline supports multiple backbones (DinoV2, Qwen-VL) and strategies (LoRA, Backbone, Head-only) to find the optimal balance between accuracy and compute.Quick linksCode: fine_tune.py{: .btn .btn-outline .mr-2 }Code: lorafinetune.py{: .btn .btn-outline .mr-2 }Code: dinov2.py{: .btn .btn-outline }Training StrategiesWe experimented with three distinct levels of model adaptation:<div class="bs-cards"><div class="bs-card"><h3>Head-Only</h3><p>We keep the vision backbone frozen and only train a small MLP classification head. This is the fastest method but relies entirely on the pre-trained model's existing knowledge.</p></div><div class="bs-card"><h3>LoRA (Qwen-VL)</h3><p>Using <b>Low-Rank Adaptation</b>, we inject trainable rank-decomposition matrices into the transformer layers. This enables VLM adaptation with 10x less VRAM and training time.</p></div><div class="bs-card"><h3>Backbone Tuning (DinoV2)</h3><p>We unfreeze the vision encoder and train it alongside the head using a tiered learning rate (e.g., 5e-6 for backbone, 1e-4 for head). This yielded our best results.</p></div></div>Technical ImplementationOptimized QuantizationTo handle Large Vision Models (LVMs) like Qwen3-VL on consumer hardware (11GB VRAM), we implemented 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization. This reduced VRAM usage from ~5GB down to ~2GB without significant loss in classification precision.Contiguous Label MappingWhile our dataset stores raw FEN-style piece IDs (0-16), our classifier operates on a contiguous 13-class index. We use a look-up table (LUT) to map these during training, covering:Empty squaresWhite pieces (Pawn to King)Black pieces (Pawn to King){: .decision }Why DinoV2? Our testing showed that self-supervised models like DinoV2 outperform generic VLMs for fine-grained discrimination (like distinguishing a White King from a White Queen on a low-res tile). DinoV2's patch-based architecture is naturally suited for the geometric regularity of chessboards.Performance ResultsOur experiments compared different backbones and strategies using Balanced Accuracy and F1 Score to account for the naturally imbalanced distribution of pieces (e.g., many empty squares and pawns, few kings).{: .highlight }The Winner: DinoV2 (Backbone) achieved a remarkable 95.78% balanced accuracy, significantly outperforming the head-only and LoRA-based VLM approaches.ModelStrategyBalanced AccF1 ScoreVal LossDinoV2 (Backbone)backbone95.78%95.63%0.1720DinoV2 (Head Only)head-only75.24%74.09%0.7692Qwen2-VL (LoRA)lora77.82%78.16%0.7651Qwen2-VL (Head Only)head-only68.89%69.46%0.9517Key TakeawaysUnfreezing the backbone is critical: For DinoV2, moving from head-only to backbone tuning increased accuracy by over 20%.LoRA vs. Head-only: In Qwen-VL, LoRA provided a ~9% boost over simple head training with minimal compute overhead.Efficiency: DinoV2 remains the most efficient choice for inference, providing the highest accuracy with the lowest latency.{% include finetuning_stats.md %}
